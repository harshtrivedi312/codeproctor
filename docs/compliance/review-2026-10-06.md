Status: **DRAFT for owner approval.** Reviewed 2026-10-06 by the compliance reviewer. Approver: Harsh Trivedi. Reviewed: `decisions.md`, `legal-brief.md`, `consent-document.md` (v0.3), `dpia.md` (v0.2), `retention-schedule.md` (v0.3), `processors.md` (v0.2), `volunteer-consent-form.md` (v0.1); ADR 0004 (including the section 9 amendment), ADR 0002; `docs/fsd.md`; the code at `e075967f` (SDK detectors, retention service, candidate retention page, consent seed).

## Summary

1. The documents are carefully built and mostly consistent with each other. The biggest weakness is not drafting: it is places where the text promises something the system does not (or does not yet) do.
2. Top mismatch: the consent document says face data is used "only to confirm your identity", but the browser's gaze, face-count and presence detectors also analyse face landmarks during the test (BIPA statutory-damages risk).
3. The biometric consent is not yet "freely given" in the way the GDPR and the ICO expect: face matching is "required", and the alternative is described as a case-by-case favour. Withdrawal also does not stop retention of the biometric data.
4. Several legal dates in the DPIA are out of date (EU AI Act, Colorado AI Act, UK automated-decision rules). One new rule (California's 4-year ADS record retention) conflicts with the 1-year results limit (C-26).
5. Six blockers, ten should-fix items (S1 to S10), four minor items below. Exact replacement wording is given for each.

## Blockers (pilot cannot start; B3 and B4 block the first EU/UK candidate)

### B1. The consent says biometrics are used "only to confirm your identity", but they are not  **[HIGH RISK: biometrics, statutory damages]**

- **Finding.** `consent-document.md` section 4 and section 11 item 3 limit biometric use to identity verification. But the SDK analyses face landmarks every second for GAZE_AWAY (iris offset from landmarks: `irisOffsetFromLandmarks`, used by the gaze rules in `packages/proctor-sdk/src/detectors/rules.ts` and computed in `packages/proctor-sdk/src/detectors/worker/inference.worker.ts`; `rules.test.ts` covers it), NO_FACE and MULTIPLE_FACES (FR-606, `docs/fsd.md:80`). Decision OQ-15 itself treats those detectors as face processing.
- **Why it matters.** BIPA covers a "scan of face geometry" and requires written notice of the specific purpose, and a release for that purpose. Using geometry for a purpose not in the release is a new unconsented use. Statutory damages are USD 1,000 (negligent) or 5,000 (reckless) per person, per method after SB 2979. GDPR purpose limitation and transparency (Arts. 5(1)(b), 13) are also breached. The statement is also false, which is worse than silence.
- **Options.** (a) Describe the in-test analysis and cover it by the same explicit consent. (b) Remove the detectors. (c) Make them optional. 
- **Recommendation: (a), with (c) via accommodation (already C-25).** Proposed C-36: in-test face analysis is covered by the biometric consent and described in the consent document.
- **Replacement wording, section 4 (replace the paragraph starting "Face images and face templates are biometric data"):**

  > Face images and measurements of your face are **biometric data**. We use them for two purposes only: (1) to confirm your identity, as described above, including the check every 2 minutes during the test that the person at the keyboard is still you (our server compares one small webcam frame with your verified face); and (2) during the test, to check whether you are present, whether anyone else is in view, and whether you are looking at the screen. The second purpose uses your webcam picture to measure the position of your face and eyes **in your browser**. It does not identify you, and the measurements are not stored. Only the resulting flags (for example "looked away for 5 seconds"), and sometimes a snapshot, are saved. We use biometric data for nothing else.

- **Replacement wording, section 11 item 3:**

  > you give your explicit consent and written release for Rysun Labs Inc. to collect, use, store and disclose to our hosting provider (Amazon Web Services, which stores it for us) your biometric data, meaning images of your face and measurements of your face, **only** (a) to verify your identity and (b) to check your presence and attention during this assessment, and to keep and destroy it as described in section 8 and in the retention and destruction schedule;

- **Lawyer confirmation.** Yes for the BIPA reading of landmark-based gaze analysis (courts have not settled it). Conservative treatment costs little.

### B2. The BIPA release does not cover disclosure to AWS, and the retention term is a variable  **[HIGH RISK: statutory damages]**

- **Finding.** BIPA 15(b) needs the specific purpose and length of term in the written notice; 15(d) needs consent to disclose to a third party. The release (section 11 item 3) says "collect, use and store" only and the term is "[N] days". Webcam recordings and evidence snapshots (which show the face) follow `retention_days` up to 730 days (ADR 0004 section 9; OQ-18, OQ-19 are still open).
- **Recommendation.** Proposed C-37: all face-bearing items (ID image, selfie, mismatch frames, evidence snapshots, webcam and room-scan recordings, second-camera recording) are capped at 90 days from capture (answers OQ-18 and OQ-19 as the suggested answers propose). The consent states the number "90 days", not a variable. Organisations may shorten, never extend. Cost to the owner: some reviews must finish within 90 days of capture (the 7-day appeal window plus review fits).
- **Replacement wording, section 8 first bullet:**

  > Recordings (screen, webcam, microphone, room scan and any second camera), the ID photo, the selfie, evidence images and re-check frames that didn't match: deleted **no later than 90 days after they were recorded**, or sooner if the organisation has chosen a shorter period. Keystroke data: deleted [N] days after your assessment is finished (normally 90).

  Add a matching change to `retention-schedule.md` (rows for recordings, evidence snapshots, ID, selfie and re-check frames: "no more than 90 days from capture").
- **Also fix** the retention-schedule line "never more than 90 days": it does not say from when. ADR 0004 section 9 runs the face clock from capture or submission (C-35), not from "after the assessment is finished". Today the documents say "[N] days after the assessment is finished ... and never more than 90 days". Replace with "no more than 90 days after capture".
- **Lawyer confirmation.** Not needed for the cap itself.

### B3. Withdrawing biometric consent does not stop retention  **[HIGH RISK: biometrics, Art. 9]**

- **Finding.** Section 9 ("Stop or withdraw") says that after withdrawal "data already collected is kept as described in section 8, so the attempt can be reviewed fairly".
- **Why it matters.** GDPR Art. 7(3) requires withdrawal to be as easy as giving consent, and Art. 9 data has no lawful basis once explicit consent is gone. "Fair review" is not an Art. 9(2) condition. The same applies to Colorado (withdrawal) and is a bad fact under BIPA (purpose no longer exists).
- **Options.** (a) On withdrawal, stop biometric processing and delete ID image, selfie and re-check frames within 30 days, keeping flags and results (which rest on legitimate interests). The identity check becomes "waived by withdrawal" with the video ID check of C-19. **Conflict with ADR 0015 section 6:** it allows a waiver only while no identity attempt exists, and a withdrawal after an attempt would waive after one exists. Widening that window is owner question 10 in ADR 0015, so (a) depends on it. DL-30's rule (delete every earlier attempt's sealed ID image and selfie when a waiver is set) is the deletion mechanism. (b) Keep as drafted (not defensible for EU/UK). 
- **Recommendation: (a).** Proposed C-38. It needs a small backend task: a "biometric consent withdrawn" action that runs the face-tier deletion early (BE-06/DB-06; ADR 0004 face tier already deletes independently of holds, so the machinery exists).
- **Replacement wording, section 9, "Stop or withdraw":**

  > **Stop or withdraw.** You can stop at any time by ending the session. Recording stops at once. You can also withdraw your consent to the use of your biometric data at any time, before or after the test, by using the "withdraw biometric consent" action in the candidate portal (to be built), by telling your recruiter, or by writing to privacy@example.com (ending the session during the test, or finishing the test normally, does **not** count as a withdrawal). We then stop using your biometric data and delete your ID photo, selfie and any re-check images within 30 days. Your identity can then be checked on a short video call instead. Withdrawing does not affect your assessment and does not affect anything we did before you withdrew. The rest of your data is kept as described in section 8.

- **Lawyer confirmation.** No.

### B4. Biometric consent is not freely given while face matching is "required" and the alternative is discretionary  **[HIGH RISK: biometrics; applies to the first EU/UK candidate]**

- **Finding.** C-02 makes face matching "required for every candidate" and also relies on explicit consent. Section 9 offers an alternative only as "we handle each request individually". Consent that is a condition of the service, with no equally easy alternative, is presumed not freely given (GDPR Art. 7(4), recital 43; EDPB Guidelines 05/2020; ICO guidance), and the hiring power imbalance makes it worse (DPIA R5).
- **Recommendation.** Proposed amendment to C-02 (strengthens it): the candidate may choose not to have their face matched, as of right, by contacting the recruiter before starting. The alternative is a short video ID check with the recruiter, with no penalty. The recruiter still records a reason under C-19, but the reason can be "candidate preference"; the recruiter cannot refuse. Face matching stays the default path. The consent text says so plainly.
- **Replacement wording, section 9, first bullet:**

  > **You can choose not to have your face matched.** If you prefer, tell your recruiter [name, email] before you start. Your identity is then checked on a short video call, and the face match and the face-based checks during the test are switched off. This choice has no effect on how your assessment is judged. If you need other changes (extra time, assistive technology, no webcam), ask in the same way; we will make reasonable adjustments.

  Also replace the lead sentence of section 11 item 3 with "...you give your explicit consent... (you may refuse; see section 9)". 
- **Lawyer confirmation.** Yes. My opinion, for counsel to test, is that an alternative offered on request is enough for freely given consent; counsel should confirm for Germany and France, whose authorities are strict.

### B5. The consent promises "a trained reviewer reviews every assessment", but ADR 0002 and the FSD still auto-clear

- **Finding.** Section 5 and section 11 item 5 promise review of every assessment. `docs/adr/0002...md:67` and `docs/fsd.md:131` still allow GRADED to COMPLETED with no reviewer. C-28 is decided; the changes are tracked (`docs/followups/qa.md` section 11.3; the hub; FSD section 3 is fixed in #218).
- **Recommendation.** Pilot gate: do not load the consent for use until BE-13 and BE-14 make GRADED always go to UNDER_REVIEW and hold results until sign-off, and the FSD and ADR 0002 match. Add this to the placeholder guard checklist.
- **Lawyer confirmation.** No.

### B6. The consent document describes a post-test questionnaire (section 10) that does not exist before pilot exit

- **Finding.** C-13 puts FAIR-01 before the pilot *exit* review, not the start. Section 10 and the retention row tell every pilot candidate they "may be asked" questions with a separate consent. If the form isn't built, the text is inaccurate; if it is built hastily, special-category data (ethnicity, Art. 9) would be collected on an unreviewed basis.
- **Recommendation.** Remove section 10 and its retention row from the pilot version. When FAIR-01 ships, show its own notice and consent at the point of asking and issue consent version x.y+1. This is not a weakening: the protection arrives with the feature.
- **Lawyer confirmation.** No.

## Should fix

### S1. Make biometric consent a separate required tick box

Drafting note 1 asks this. **Recommendation: yes** (Proposed C-39). GDPR explicit consent should be separate from other terms (EDPB 05/2020), and a separate box strengthens the BIPA release. Wording:

> [ ] I explicitly consent to Rysun Labs Inc. collecting and using images and measurements of my face to confirm my identity and to check my presence and attention during the test, as described in sections 4 and 8. I know I can refuse (section 9) and can withdraw at any time.

The 18+ box (C-30) stays separate. Keep the single typed-name signature (C-07).

### S2. Bystanders and other people in the room

DPIA R4 says candidates are told in advance to use a private space; the consent document does not say it. Audio of other people can also engage two-party consent rules in some US states (for example Illinois and California). Add to section 3:

> **Please take the test alone, in a private room, with other people's permission for anything they might say or do in view.** Your webcam and microphone may pick up other people and your surroundings. Close other apps and hide anything private on your screen.

### S3. Retention schedule gaps (documents promise less than the system holds)

The schedule has no row for: the candidate's name, email and invitation details; one-time-code records; accommodation notes and the waiver reason (health data; OQ-12); the audit log (ADR 0004 R-5 keeps audit rows); declined-consent records (OQ-11); the erased-candidates list kept outside backups (ADR 0004 R-7); SES's suppression list; S3 and CloudTrail access logs; and copies sent to the ATS or by webhook, which we cannot delete. The code anonymises the candidate once all their sessions pass the results marker (`apps/api/src/retention/retention.repository.ts`, `anonymiseInTx`), so the schedule can and should say so. Add rows:

| Data | How long we keep it |
| --- | --- |
| Your name, email address and invitation details | Until 1 year after your last test (when your results are deleted), then deleted or anonymised. If you declined, the record of the invitation and the decline: 3 years |
| Accommodation requests and notes | 1 year after the test, with your results; deleted on request |
| Access log (who opened your recordings and results) | [N years: owner to decide]. It holds internal reference numbers and staff IP addresses, not your recordings, answers or images |
| Copies sent to the hiring team's applicant tracking system | Under that system's own rules. We ask them to delete when you ask us to |

Recommendations: OQ-9 (logs 30 days: **accept**), OQ-10 (legal hold: **accept**; I add that the hold must stop at the end of the claim, be audited, and never extend the face tier's 90 days, which is already ADR 0004's rule), OQ-11 (declined records 3 years: **accept**), OQ-12 (clear accommodation notes on erasure: **accept**), OQ-13 (reviewers see only "identity check waived": **accept**), OQ-14 and OQ-15 (**accept**). OQ-20 (the results clock runs from the retention anchor, after review and appeal) is also a recommendation to **accept**; it matches the schedule's "when the clock starts" wording. Audit log retention is a new open item: recommended 3 years, matching consent records, with no personal content (Proposed C-40).

### S4. California's 4-year ADS record rule conflicts with the 1-year results limit (C-26)  **[needs owner decision]**

- **Finding.** C-26 (1 year) relies on the US federal 1-year hiring record rule (29 CFR 1602.14). California's FEHA regulations (in force 1 Oct 2025) require employers with 5+ employees to keep employment and ADS data four years. If Rysun Labs Inc. hires for California-based roles, deleting results at 1 year may itself breach that rule and weakens defence against a discrimination claim.
- **Options.** (A) Keep 1 year for everyone and exclude California-based roles from the pilot. (B) Keep results (scores, verdict, reviewer decision, ADS output; never recordings or face data) 4 years for roles located in California, 1 year elsewhere, set per job. (C) Keep results 4 years for everyone.
- **Recommendation: (B)** if you will hire in California, otherwise (A). It touches ADR 0004 R-5 and DB-06 (a per-job results retention setting). The consent and the schedule would then say "1 year (4 years for roles based in California)".
- **Lawyer confirmation.** Yes, ideally California counsel.

### S5. Erasure hold wording (C-06) can exceed statutory deadlines

"Within 30 days of the request, or within 30 days after an open review or appeal closes, whichever is later" has no outer limit. GDPR Art. 12(3) allows one month, extendable by up to two months only where complex, with notice and reasons within the first month. The Illinois AI Video Interview Act (if it applies) requires deletion within 30 days with no hold, other than a legal dispute. CCPA allows 45 days plus 45. The hold also rests on a weak basis: an internal review is not a "legal claim" under Art. 17(3)(e).
**Recommendation (Proposed amendment to C-06, numbered C-41):** keep the hold only for the results and reviewer notes (never face data or recordings, which ADR 0004 already deletes without a hold), limit it to at most 60 days after the request, and say so. Wording:

> Deletion is completed within 30 days of your request. If a review or appeal of your assessment is still open, we delete your recordings, images and keystroke data within the 30 days anyway, and we may keep your results and the reviewer's notes until the review or appeal ends, and no more than 60 days after your request. We tell you if this applies. Unless a legal claim is open, no longer.

### S6. Article 13 information missing from the consent document

Add: the identity of the EU/UK representatives (if appointed, see S9); the interests relied on (already section 5a) and the right to object (present); that providing data is necessary to take the assessment, and the consequence of not providing it (you cannot take it online; see the alternative); meaningful information about the logic of the risk score (what it uses, that it only prioritises a person's review); how to get a copy of the transfer safeguards; the right to complain to a supervisory authority (present for EU/UK; add the UK ICO by name). Suggested paragraph for section 5:

> **How the risk score works.** The software combines the flags it raised (for example, large pastes or looking away) into a score that helps the reviewer see which parts of your session to look at first. It never decides anything, does not change your test score, and a reviewer sees your whole session whatever the score is.

*(Check that this statement is true of the final risk design: ADR 0001, integrity track. If the reviewer's queue is ordered by the score, "look at first" is right; "whatever the score is" depends on C-28.)*

### S6a. Section 7 transfer wording overstates what SCCs do

If Rysun Labs Inc. is a US-only controller collecting data directly (DPIA section 8; EDPB Guidelines 05/2021), SCCs with AWS and others protect onward processing; they are not the protection for the candidate's own submission to us. Replacement for section 7, second sentence onward:

> If you are in the European Union or the United Kingdom, your data will be sent to and stored in the United States. The law of the US may give you fewer rights than the law where you live. We protect it with encryption, strict access controls and short retention, and our service providers are bound by data processing agreements that include the European Commission's Standard Contractual Clauses (and, for the UK, the UK Addendum). You can ask privacy@example.com for a copy.

### S7. Legal notices that must reach the candidate before the consent page

The consent page appears after the invitation, but some laws require earlier notice: NYC Local Law 144 (10 business days before use, if it applies); the Illinois AI Video Interview Act (explanation before the interview, and consent); Illinois HB 3773 (notice of AI use). Put a notice in the invitation email and in job postings. Suggested text for both:

> This role includes a proctored online coding assessment. Software flags possible integrity issues, and also analyses your webcam picture to check your presence and attention and, with your consent, to confirm your identity with a face match. Nothing is rejected automatically: a person reviews every assessment and makes every hiring decision. You can ask for an alternative to the face match, or for accommodations, by contacting [recruiter]. Full details are in the consent document you will be asked to read and sign before anything is recorded: [link]. Retention schedule: [link].

This is for the invitation template (BE-06) and the job posting (owner; C-14).

### S8. Update the DPIA's legal statements (section 7 and 3)

- EU AI Act: replace the "Timing" bullet with "Under Regulation (EU) 2026/1744 (in force 27 July 2026), Annex III obligations apply from 2 December 2027. Article 5 (including emotion recognition) has applied since 2 February 2025." Keep the recommendation to start the AI Act file now. Add that no detector, including the keystroke analytics, may infer emotion or stress.
- UK: replace "reworks the UK rules ... verify" with "Articles 22A to 22D UK GDPR have applied since 5 February 2026; a significant decision may not be based solely on automated processing without safeguards. - Colorado: replace the Colorado AI Act paragraph with SB 26-189 (effective 1 January 2027), and add the Colorado biometric amendment (HB 24-1130), which applies today to employers and applicants.
- Illinois: HB 3773 has applied since 1 January 2026; IDHR rules withdrawn on 2 June 2026.
- California: CCPA ADMT duties from 1 January 2027 (pre-use notice, opt-out or human appeal, risk assessment); biometric identity verification requires a risk assessment; the FEHA regulations apply since 1 October 2025 (see S4).
- Add the CCPA "notice at collection" requirement to the consent document: categories (identifiers, biometric information, audio and visual information, internet activity, employment-related information, sensitive personal information), purposes, retention (section 8) and that we neither sell nor share. Section 9 already has the last point; add the categories sentence to section 3.

### S9. EU and UK representatives (Art. 27) and the DPO  **[decision needed]**

The monitoring of people in the EU/UK (Art. 3(2)(b)) makes the GDPR apply to a US-only controller. The Art. 27(2)(a) exemption needs processing that is occasional, not large-scale special-category data, and unlikely to risk rights. Biometric ID plus recording of home environments makes "unlikely to risk" hard to claim. Options: (A) appoint EU and UK representatives (low-cost services exist) before the first EU/UK candidate; (B) start the pilot with US-only candidates and defer EU/UK; (C) rely on the exemption (not recommended). **Recommendation: (A) or (B).** A DPO is probably not mandatory (Art. 37 requires large-scale systematic monitoring or special-category data as a core activity; a hiring pilot is unlikely). Record the reasoning in the DPIA.

### S10. Transfer mechanism: lean on SCCs, not on DPF alone

DPF still stands, but C-703/25 P is pending. Recommendation (Proposed C-42): in `processors.md`, make the signed DPA with SCCs (and the UK Addendum) the primary mechanism for every processor, and record DPF as secondary. The owner must check the live DPF list for AWS and Cloudflare and save the DPAs and sub-processor lists.
